Building a reproducible vuln lab you actually trust

2026-10-17 · Ishan Uprety · 8 min read

If you claim to measure the cost of safety rails in automated vulnerability discovery, the first question a careful reader should ask is: measured against what? A one-off demo on a thrown-together target is not a result. A screenshot of a finding against someone else's infrastructure is not a result. A result is something another researcher can re-run and get the same shape of answer.

That means the lab is not a side detail of the research. It is the research substrate. If the range is fuzzy, every number that comes out of it is fuzzy. This post is about what it takes to build a deliberately-vulnerable range I would actually trust — and that I would ask a reviewer to trust.

Why self-owned is non-negotiable

There is a temptation, especially when the work touches bug-bounty culture, to treat live programs as the "real" evaluation surface. They are real in the sense that they have production complexity. They are the wrong surface for measuring a research system.

Live targets move. Authorization windows expire. Rate limits change. Findings cannot be published as evaluation fixtures. You cannot dial enforcement up and down on a third-party system just to plot a trade-off curve. And you certainly cannot invite another lab to reproduce your numbers against someone else's assets.

The only honest test range for this work is one you own, can version, can destroy, and can rebuild from a known commit.

Self-owned does not mean "a VM on my laptop with DVWA and hope." It means a containerized set of deliberately vulnerable applications whose topology, versions, and seeded flaws are under the same version control as the harness that runs against them. When the range drifts, the commit history says so.

What "reproducible" has to mean

People say "reproducible lab" and mean "I can start the containers again." That is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. For a thesis that claims to measure enforcement cost, reproducibility has to cover the whole experimental loop:

  • Version-controlled configs. Image pins, compose files, seed data, and network topology live in git. A run is identified by a commit SHA of the range and a commit SHA of the harness — not by "whatever was on disk Tuesday."
  • Same harness, known knobs. The discovery engine that exercises the range is the same binary (or the same resolved dependency set) every time. Rail strictness is a dial with named levels, not a pile of ad-hoc flags someone remembered to set.
  • Rails dialed up and down on purpose. The point of the lab is the comparison: strict enforcement versus relaxed enforcement on an otherwise identical run. If you cannot flip that dial cleanly, you do not have a trade-off experiment — you have a story.
  • Re-runnable by others. A stranger with the repo, a container runtime, and the documented entrypoint should be able to reproduce the curve within noise. If that requires my personal environment, it is not reproducible.

None of this is exotic infrastructure. It is the same discipline experimental sciences have always required, applied to a security-research range that happens to be made of containers.

Deliberately vulnerable, deliberately boring

The targets themselves should be boring in the right ways. Known vulnerability classes, known ground truth, enough surface to exercise authorization, scope, rate, and blast-radius gates — and no more drama than that. Fancy zero-days are the wrong fixture; you cannot score coverage against a mystery.

What the range does need is structural variety: different auth models, different trust boundaries, enough network shape that a scope gate has something real to enforce. A flat single-app lab under-tests the rails. A zoo of unrelated apps over-tests nothing and makes the curve unreadable. The design problem is choosing a small set that stresses the enforcement layer without turning the evaluation into folklore.

The ethics line that makes the lab credible

This is the part that is easy to skip in a build log and fatal to skip in a thesis about responsible automation.

Nothing live. Nothing third-party. The evaluation never points the harness at a production system, a bug-bounty asset, or anyone else's lab without an explicit, recorded authorization that is not part of this measurement story. The measurement story runs on the self-owned range. Full stop.

That is not a purity ritual. It is load-bearing for the claim. If you test "safe automation" by occasionally going unsafe, you have undercut the thing you said you were measuring. The architectural rails exist so the engine cannot overstep; the lab exists so you can prove what those rails cost without needing to overstep in order to find out.

Responsible automation has to be tested responsibly — or the numbers are about a different system than the one you claim to have built.

What trust looks like in practice

A lab I trust is one where I can answer, from artifacts alone:

  • Which range commit and harness commit produced this curve?
  • Which rail level was active, and is that level encoded as data rather than as a prose recollection?
  • Did any action escape a gate? (The audit log answers; hope does not.)
  • Can a reviewer rebuild and re-run without pinging me?

If any of those answers is hand-wavy, the lab is not ready to support a claim about safety cost. Getting those answers to be boring and mechanical is most of the work.
